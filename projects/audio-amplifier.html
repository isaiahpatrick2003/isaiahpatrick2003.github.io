Stereo Audio Amplifier PCB

An LM386-based amplifier taken through simulation, schematic capture, PCB layout, physical assembly, and functional bench testing.

Analog DesignLTspiceKiCadPCB LayoutSoldering
Design toolsLTspice · KiCad
Core hardware3 × LM386
Target load4 Ω speakers
OutcomeWorking PCB
Overview

From circuit idea to working hardware

The objective was to build a stereo audio amplifier with independent volume control and enough output swing to drive 4-ohm speakers. I developed and checked the circuit in LTspice, recreated it in KiCad, assigned footprints, placed components, routed traces, soldered the board, and tested the completed hardware.

The challenge
  • Translate an electrical design into a manufacturable PCB.
  • Keep inputs, power, amplifier stages, and outputs organized.
  • Select footprints that match the physical through-hole parts.
  • Verify operation with a function generator after assembly.
Engineering workflow

Design Process

Each stage reduced a different category of risk before the design became physical hardware.

01

Simulation in LTspice

I began with an electrical model of the amplifier. This established the expected signal behavior and gave me a reference before committing to a schematic and PCB.

Changing a simulated circuit is inexpensive; changing a fabricated board is not. Starting here reduced the likelihood of carrying a basic design error into hardware.

02

Schematic Capture in KiCad

I translated the simulation into a structured KiCad schematic containing the input terminals, two volume potentiometers, three LM386 op-amps, coupling and bypass capacitors, speaker outputs, power input, switch, and indicator LED.

Assigning every symbol to a physical footprint connected the electrical design to the parts that would actually be soldered.

03

PCB Placement and Routing

I arranged the through-hole components so the input side, controls, amplifier stages, power input, and speaker outputs were easy to identify and access.

The routed traces followed the schematic netlist. Connector placement and readable signal flow mattered because the board needed to be assembled, wired, adjusted, and tested by hand.

04

Design-Rule Check

Before fabrication, I used KiCad's design-rule checker to review electrical connections, copper clearances, footprint placement, and silkscreen issues.

This check helped identify problems that could cause manufacturing defects, shorts, or difficult assembly before the board was produced.

05

3D Inspection

I used KiCad's 3D viewer to verify that the selected footprints matched the intended through-hole components and that tall parts had enough physical clearance.

The model also helped confirm access to the screw terminals, potentiometers, switch, and mounting hardware before fabrication.

06

Assembly and Soldering

After the PCB was produced, I installed and soldered every through-hole component, including the LM386 packages, resistors, capacitors, potentiometers, terminal blocks, switch, LED, and mounting hardware.

The completed board matched the KiCad layout and worked without requiring a redesign.

07

Bench Testing

I drove the board with a function generator. Changing frequency produced different audible tones, while changing amplitude adjusted the output volume.

The final demonstration confirmed that the simulated and laid-out design had successfully become functional hardware.

  • Board powered successfully
  • Audible output from the speaker connection
  • Frequency changed the output tone
  • Amplitude controlled perceived volume
Engineering reflection

What the project taught me

The most important lesson was that a PCB project is a process. Simulation, schematic capture, footprint selection, layout, assembly, and testing each expose different problems.

Once the design became physical, practical decisions mattered as much as the circuit itself: connector access, component orientation, spacing, readable signal flow, and testability.

If I built this again

I would record quantitative measurements more systematically, including gain across frequency, input and output amplitude, supply current, and distortion. I would also explore a smaller layout while preserving serviceability and clear routing.
